6:47 AM — Maya Okafor —

Third Year Medical Student 

Maya Okafor hasn’t set an alarm in years. Her watch knows her sleep 

architecture better than she does. It vibrated at 6:31 this morning because 

today’s session requires fine motor precision, and her recovery metrics say 

she’s ready. 

She’s a third-year medical student, though that phrase means something 

different now. There are no semesters. Maya moves through competency gates. 

Some students reach this point in two and a half years. Some take four. 

Her digital profile tracks everything. Three weeks ago, the system noticed her 

confidence drops when she encounters pediatric cardiac cases. It didn’t flag 

her. It assembled a sequence of simulation exercises, graded from gentle to 

genuinely hard, and opened slots in her calendar. 

She passes the Sim Gym on the fourth floor — two residents running through a 

robotic suturing sequence on their own time. On the landing, she passes the 

room everyone calls the Porch. The Porch is the building’s answer to questions 

medicine took a very long time to ask: where do you put the feelings? How do you 

prioritize physician mental health and wellness. 

The suite doesn’t look like a sim lab. That was deliberate, made by a firm that 

had built environments for museums and immersive theater. The room she enters 

is a pediatric emergency department. Mid-shift. A half-eaten sandwich on the 

nursing station. Spatial audio layered: a parent’s voice from behind the door. 

Maya’s patient is a four-year-old named Luca, a dinosaur backpack on the 

chair, a father who keeps asking if they can just go home. The scenario was 

generated overnight. Faculty approved it yesterday with a review that took 

eleven minutes. 

Maya doesn’t know any of this. She knows a child is struggling to breathe, and 

a father is scared, and the nurse is waiting for her to make a call. It feels real.

# Voices, Research and Insights

*Who we learned from, what their strongest thoughts were, and the three insights the client brought forward for concepting. Tagged throughout: where our research went beyond what the medical simulation field could arrive at on its own.*

## The Voices

Twenty two primary interviews across medicine, the military, elite athletics, simulation leadership, futures research, and immersive design. These are the people whose thinking shaped this work, and the ideas of theirs that held up under synthesis. Each voice is listed with a short profile, then the one or two thoughts that most directly informed the center’s design.

**A note on the tags.** *Beyond expert consensus* marks thinking that the cross-industry or futures research surfaced. *Within the field* marks thinking the field already knows, which our research organized or confirmed.

### Dr. James Korndorffer, Jr.

*Vice Dean of Education, Dell Medical School. *

Dr. Korndorffer built simulation centers at Tulane and Stanford before coming to Dell Med. His background is surgical. His orientation is visionary. He gave the Design in Health team to focus outside of the spaces he was already familiar, to think big and to consider all possibilities for the future based on trends we saw in analogous industries.

*Two standout ideas from his interview: *

Flexibility is the non-negotiable lesson. What gets built in three to five years is not what will be needed five years after that. The center has to absorb change without capital-intensive rebuilds.

Current VR simulation often fails at environmental realism. The technology has not caught up to the conditions. *The reframe, that this is a narrative design problem rather than a technology fidelity problem, comes from analogous research. *

### Dr. Alisa Coker

*Director of Robotic Surgery and Education, Dell Medical School.*

Coker is the operational ground truth of this project. She currently trains residents on borrowed equipment at Ascension hospitals, and negotiates access. She gave great insight into the challenges associated with scheduling constraint.

She spent time at both John’s Hopkins and UCSD center with tremendous lessons from their sim centers. Built capability and executed capability are different problems, and the second one is harder.

*The cross-industry evidence, specifically aviation mandates and military protected time, reframes this from a technology adoption question into an institutional commitment question.*

### Dr. Ryan Broderick

*Director, Center for the Future of Surgery, UC San Diego.*

Broderick runs a highly effective simulation center. One thousand labs a year. Seventy percent funded by industry partnerships. Apple Vision Pro research in live surgery. His voice is the working operator’s voice, and his model is the one medical simulation currently has.

The industry partnership model is the funding model. Eight million dollars of in-kind equipment from Stryker alone. Without device-company sponsors, it is unlikely UCSD exist in its current form and speaks to an important dependency for planning. *Within the field.*

Haptics remain unbeaten for procedural skills. The Vision Pro is useful for pre-operative planning and remote consultation. Dr. Broderick suggested he believed it could be five to ten years away from replacing the hands-on operating room, and industry investment will set the timeline. *Within the field.*

### Peter W. Singer

*Futurist and author. Advisor to Hollywood, the CIA, and Pentagon. Described by the Wall Street Journal as the premier futurist in the national security environment.*

Singer sits outside medicine entirely. That is the primary reason we sought to speak with him. He thinks for a living about narrative architecture, human-AI teaming, and what simulation fails to prepare people for.

There are four concurrent AI relationships, not a linear progression. AI as decision aid, AI as remote presence, AI as teammate, AI as agent. A clinician graduating in 2035 will face all four. Medical simulation currently trains for the first one.

*This reframes AI readiness from a technology adoption timeline into a curriculum architecture question.*

He provided examples of scenarios that landed as “hokey and sterile” and identified them as a narrative design problem, not a technology fidelity problem. His colleague who designed the Abraham Lincoln Presidential Museum made immersion work through story architecture. Medical simulation traditionally hires technologists and educators. It is less likely to hire experience designers, narrative architects, or spatial audio engineers.

*The talent-pipeline reframe is the sharpest cross-industry contribution in the research. The field should consider hiring talent from non-traditional industries to best shape learner experience.*

General (Ret.) John "Mike" Murray

*First Commanding General, U.S. Army Futures Command. Four decades of leadership in high stakes training and life on the line environments. *

GEN Murray stood up a four-star command from nothing in the middle of downtown Austin. His lens is institutional. How training systems absorb change, how teams beat individuals, how leaders learn from failure.

He shared the overall futility of perfectly predicting the future but the value of design for wide baselines and adaptability instead. In his own words, predicting the future is easy; predicting it well is not, and one thing he will guarantee is that the prediction will be wrong.

*This posture is the counterweight to the architect’s instinct to over-specify the 2030 facility now.*

Failure is the best teacher, and team competition outperforms individual competition. Environments designed to fail safely build resilience. The widgets and gadgets are only as effective as the human behind them.

*Strong advocation for team-competition design. Dell Med leadership shares the aspiration; the military has the operational playbook.*

### Lieutenant General (Ret.) Karen H. Gibson

*Former U.S. Senate Sergeant at Arms. Thirty-three-year Army intelligence career, including Deputy Director of National Intelligence and Director of Intelligence for U.S. Central Command.*

Gibson ran a 150-person intelligence team in Korea across multiple specialties, clearance levels, and time zones. She later led U.S. Senate security during a period of extraordinary institutional stress. Her thinking is structural and across a wide spectrum of considerations i.e. How teams move information, how training progresses, how to navigate human burnout and vulnerability. She also provided insights on risks, in particular how vetting of a vendor or subcontractor is as, if not more, important than internal security. 

She shared insights on the military’s training model of crawl, walk, run as structural progression. Foundational understanding comes before role specialization. Role specialization comes before information flow design. Information flow comes before emergency drills. AI arrives should arguably arrive later in the progression. She uses the calculator analogy: you teach the math before you hand over the shortcut.

*This gives the AI-generated scenario pipeline its pedagogical spine.*

Data governance is a vendor and third-party security problem from day one. Her biggest challenge at the Capitol was vendors and third parties who also had access to the data. A data prioritization scheme determines what partners can touch, and it needs to be written before the partnerships are signed.

*Medical simulation has not imported this framework. Sports (NBA, NFLPA) and national intelligence have had some initial successes worth deeper study of. *

### Lieutenant Colonel Nick Rinaldi

*U.S. Army Applications Laboratory. Kinesiology and education background. Works on how soldiers are trained, measured, and modernized, including emerging work on simulation telemetry and biometric capture.*

Rinaldi is the operator’s voice on the data problem. He has sat inside Army simulators for hours, watched the system record every trigger pull and eye-track, then watched the system miss opportunities to collect valuable training data. His lens is practical. If you are going to measure, you have to keep it, tie it to a person, and use it to tell them something true about themselves over time.

Rich performance data gets generated in every simulation every day and gets discarded at the end of the session. In his own words: I am not player one. I am Rinaldi dot mil. Put it in my system. Tell me how good I am over time. Tell me I am bad at shooting. Maybe I should not be shooting a rifle anymore. The capture is solved. The persistence, the attribution, and the honest feedback loop are not.

*Beyond expert consensus. This is the Dell Med data-governance argument in its most compact form, delivered from inside an institution that has the same problem and has not solved it either. It pairs cleanly with Gibson on vendor governance, Coker on the Hopkins stat-card attempt, and the NBA and NFLPA frameworks.*

Individual capture only matters if it rolls up into a team profile with clear parameters the team has agreed on. Works well with others has to be measurable on a spectrum, not a vibe. His roster-card metaphor for a pre-med-through-graduate learner profile is the sharpest articulation in the research of what a digital learner twin actually feels like from the inside.

### *Beyond expert consensus. Sports and military training have built this. Medicine has the raw data inside robotic surgery systems and has not assembled it into a profile anyone owns.*Helen Schlachtenhaufen

*Nike-sponsored professional middle-distance runner. 2024 U.S. Olympic Trials finalist, 1500m.*

Schlachtenhaufen is the third leg of the high-stakes triangle, the one that is neither medicine nor military. Elite athletics provides a live-performance lens on what daily assessment feels like from the inside.

Progression requires getting wrecked daily. Her first year out of college, she dreaded every practice because every day she got beaten. That was where the improvement came from. Controlled, repeated exposure to pressure above current capacity.

Intrinsic motivation survives only if the rare flow-state moments are protected. Most of the work, even at Olympic level, is not fun. The few moments of flow carry the rest. The design implication is that the center has to create those moments, not just grind reps.

*Medical simulation leaders understand burnout. They do not typically frame its antidote as engineering for flow or proper recovery or resilience practices. *

### Dr. Kimberly Brown

*Dell Medical School non-invasive surgeon and educator.*

Dr. Brown gave the research great insights on some of the critical foundational challenges.

The field does not share a mental model of the resident it wants to graduate. In her words: we still don’t have a shared mental model of what kind of medical student we want in our residency program. And then how do we train, measure, and assess that. 

*Valuable as a direct internal confirmation that the gap is real.*

Relationship-based care moments are where outcomes are actually made. Not the next wonder drug. The institutional investment should be there.

## Selected by the Client

The above research and insights were were presented to the Enterprise Project Management Office in March 2026. After review by Dr. Alisa Coker, Edwin Rodriguez, and Cassandra Fleming, three insights were brought forward for deliverable development. Insight 1 was confirmed as the primary scope for concepting. Insights 2 and 3 were held as the connected territory the final deliverable should continue to reference.

### Insight 1 — Primary scope

**A paired insight.** Simulation has been optimized for the conditions it can reproduce and under-designed for the conditions that determine learning transfer. Relatedly: the most sophisticated simulation capability in the world sits unused when the institution cannot assemble the right team in the same room at the same time.

*How might we* design an immersive learning and assessment environment that clinicians fight to experience, and that is worth the cost of showing up?

This became the primary scope. It pairs two gaps the EPMO already felt, the one about fidelity and the one about access, and names them as one design problem rather than two operational headaches. The narrative-design argument and the talent-pipeline argument both live here. This is where the cross-industry research does the most intellectual work.

*Primarily Beyond expert consensus. Singer (narrative architecture), FLAIM (multi-sensory design), military (protected training time), aviation (regulatory mandates) all contributed evidence the field does not currently integrate.*

### Insight 2 — Held as connected territory

The center’s reach is not determined by its square footage.

*How might we* give architects and faculty a clear framework for what belongs in the center, what works better distributed, and what can happen anywhere, before square footage gets locked?

The hub, spoke, and anywhere framework. The EPMO flagged it as architect-ready and time-sensitive. The framework has to land before the conceptual design phase closes its assumptions. Maritime cloud simulation (Wartsila), oil-and-gas rig-side simulators, military joint operations, and Pfizer manufacturing all supplied working models. No medical voice was going to hand the EPMO this rubric.

*Almost entirely Beyond expert consensus.*

### Insight 3 — Held as connected territory

Technology shifts clinical tasks, and trust with technology shifts with them. Each shift reshapes the training and the space around it.

*How might we* design adaptable spaces and training that can be continuously refreshed as technology changes clinical work, so clinicians learn when to trust, verify, and override technology safely?

This insight carries Singer’s four-pathway AI framework and Murray’s futures posture into the design conversation. The EPMO identified it as the insight that keeps the center from being obsolete at opening.

*Beyond expert consensus in the futures framing. Within the field in the local examples such as Korndorffer on holograms and Broderick on Vision Pro.*

### A note on how the two parts connect

The three selected insights are not evenly weighted in the research. Insight 1 is where the cross-industry voices push hardest against the conventional medical simulation brief. Insight 2 is where the distributed-training models from maritime, oil and gas, and manufacturing reframe the problem entirely. Insight 3 is where the futures research, most of it Beyond the field, gives the EPMO permission to design for a world that does not yet exist. The Voices section shows who gave the research that leverage.

**3 Tech Readiness Framework**

## What It Is

A decision framework mapping five levels of human-AI relationship in simulation rooms from zero-tech "Unplugged" to "AI as Peer" embodied spaces against concrete architectural specs: power, square footage, acoustics, floor reinforcement, data infrastructure, and HVAC. It tells facilities planners and lead architects exactly what flexibility costs and what rigidity risks.

## Why This Concept Matters

The matrix sits at the intersection of the three EPMO-prioritized insights:

- **Insight 1A/1B** (under-designed conditions + team assembly): Each AI level implies a different team configuration. The matrix lets architects design rooms that serve multiple team modes.

- **Insight 2** (reach beyond square footage): The matrix operationalizes this a center with mostly Level 2-3 rooms and only a few Level 4-5 rooms is more future-ready than one betting everything on Level 5.

- **Insight 3** (technology shifts reshape training/space): This IS the deliverable that makes Insight 3 actionable for architects. Without it, the insight is philosophy. With it, it becomes a spec sheet.

It also directly translates the "Design the Pipes, Not the Faucets" principle into floor plan decisions.

## Primary Audience

**Facilities Planners and Lead Architects** with a secondary read for EPMO and AEC leadership who need to understand cost implications and risk profiles of flexibility decisions.

**Front The Matrix:**

- Five columns (Unplugged to AI as Peer), color-coded intensity bands, icons

- Rows: square footage range, power (watts/sqft), data conduit specs, acoustic treatment, HVAC capacity, structural reinforcement, cost premium over base

- A "minimum viable flexibility" callout: what infrastructure to install in ALL rooms regardless of intended level

**Back Decision Logic:**

- Decision tree: learning objective to team size to tech dependency to recommended room level

- "Common Mistakes" sidebar (over-specified rooms that waste investment, under-specified rooms that become stranded assets)

- 2030/2035/2040 timeline showing how room-level distribution shifts as tech matures (connects to Building the System hub/spoke/anywhere concept)

## Content Architecture

**The Five Levels:** Deepen each level with architectural spec tables, example training scenarios grounded in research interviews (Coker's haptics = Level 1-2, Gibson's team crisis = Level 2-3, Korndorffer's AI assessment = Level 3-4), and one cross-industry precedent per level.

**"****Room Zero****"**** Minimum Viable Flexibility:** The most strategically important section. Answers: what do we install in EVERY room at build time so we are never trapped? A one-page baseline spec drawn from Korndorffer's flexibility mandate, Murray's "you'll be wrong" principle, and Broderick's facilities pain points.

**Temporal Overlay (2030 to 2040):** 2030 opening = mostly Level 1-3, few Level 4, one Level 5 showcase. 2035 = software/sensor upgrades push rooms up without construction. 2040 = hub holds only Level 4-5 physical requirements, Level 1-3 migrates to spokes and "anywhere."

**Decision Tree and Warning Signs:** Practical planning-meeting tool with cost-of-retrofit vs. cost-of-over-provision economic argument.

**RESEARCH AND CONTENT TO VISUALIZE:**

**I. THE CORE PROBLEM THE MATRIX SOLVES**

The EPMO is designing a simulation center that opens in ~2030 and needs to serve learners through 2050+. The fundamental tension: specific technology choices made now will be obsolete by opening day, but architectural decisions (power, data, structural) are locked in at construction. The matrix translates this tension into actionable infrastructure specs.

Three EPMO-prioritized insights anchor the concept:

**Insight 1A:** Simulation has been under-designed for the conditions that maximize learning transfer.

**Insight 1B:** The most sophisticated simulation capability in the world sits unused when the institution can't assemble the right team in the same room at the same time.

**Insight 2:** The center's reach is not determined by its square footage.

**Insight 3:** Technology shifts clinical tasks and trust with technology. Each shift reshapes the training and the space around it.

The Direction for Delivery statement from the concepting workshop: Create a future-ready, scalable simulation ecosystem that prioritizes effective and efficient learning transfer and accessibility, ensuring that training is not constrained by space or synchronous availability of personnel, and can continuously adapt as technology evolves.

### II. THE FIVE LEVELS, EVIDENCE BASE

The five levels of human-AI relationship in a simulation room each carry distinct research grounding, training examples, cross-industry precedent, and architectural specifications. The levels also degrade gracefully: every room above Level 1 must be able to step down to the level below it.

**Level 1: Unplugged, Direct Human Interaction (Baseline)**

Direct human-to-human practice with no technology in the room. Standardized patient encounters, team communication drills, stress inoculation. The learner's own autonomic response is the training surface.

Training examples: Standardized patient encounters with a trained actor playing a patient (the learner practices a history, a hard conversation, a breaking-bad-news script, with nothing on the table but a chart). Team communication drills with nurse, resident, and attending talking through a deteriorating case around a table (closed-loop communication, read-backs, role clarity). Stress inoculation with time pressure, noise, and interruptions layered onto a cognitive task.

Research voices:
- SOF Trainer: "We don't have to get so complicated... incredibly expensive, hard to manage, lots of hardware. It dates quickly." Team orchestration practice "can be on a plastic doll, it doesn't really matter."
- Gibson: Design scenarios that deliberately remove technology to build foundational skills. Power-out resilience is what happens when the tech fails.
- Military Strategic Theme, Foundation-First Technology Layering: Advanced technology must be strictly sequenced to occur only after learners demonstrate mastery in analog environments. Introducing assistive technology too early creates dependency and erodes cognitive resilience.
- AMA Panel (SXSW): Medical student forces himself to complete diagnosis independently before using AI, citing concern about cognitive deskilling. "Conservative approach: add AI familiarity, don't remove core knowledge yet."
- Nurses as Firewall (SXSW): Hurricane Helene caused 300+ mile power outages requiring paper charting. Ascension's ransomware attack left clinical systems down for a month. When technology fails, analog competency is the firewall.

Cross-industry precedent (canonical):
- US Military: Tabletop exercises remain the backbone of command decision training. No screen, no simulation. People sit around a table and talk through a scenario that gets worse as it goes. A Special Operations trainer interviewed for this project noted that the practice of who gives what to whom can happen on a plastic doll.
- Gibson, 1968: Stress inoculation research showed you can train people to perform under extreme pressure with a clipboard and a timer. The rooms with no technology in them are not leftovers; they are where people learn to communicate and stay composed.

Architectural specs: Power 5 W/sqft (standard outlets). Data: basic networking, wall outlets. Square footage minimal. Acoustics standard. HVAC standard. Structural standard. Cost premium 0% (baseline).

Why Level 1 is permanent: Cognitive deskilling (the AMA panel's GPS-dependency analogy) plus AI system failures (Helene, Ascension) make analog competency the firewall, not a legacy holdover. Every room above Level 1 must be able to function as an Unplugged room. This is a patient safety requirement, not a design convenience.

**Level 2: Augmented, Human Oversees AI (+10-15%)**

Technology enhances sensory realism and provides ambient feedback without replacing human judgment. Portable, point-of-need assistive tech. Simulation backpacks.

Training examples: AR-enhanced patient encounters where a standardized patient wears AR cues and the learner sees overlaid vitals, pupil dilation, or a rash without pulling the human out of the room. Ambient intelligence feedback where ceiling mics and cameras quietly time the encounter, flag interruptions, and surface missed cues for debrief (the AI never speaks). Sensor-based communication assessment where wearables track proximity, eye-gaze direction, and speaking turns during a team drill, with data showing up in the post-scenario review rather than in the moment.

Research voices:
- Broderick (UCSD): Deployed simulation backpacks at remote sites because learners can't always come to the center.
- Dell Students Theme, Hybridized Human-Digital Interaction: Current VR implementations rejected due to poor UX, nausea, and cognitive load. Future model should prioritize AR or hybrid approaches where technology enhances rather than replaces human interaction.
- Liana Chen (Dell 1st year): Dell Med lacks modern simulation tech compared to other schools, but acknowledged value of learning foundations without tech first.
- Secondary research, ambient intelligence: Sensors and microphones embedded in patient rooms can capture encounters and provide automated assessment of communication skills, empathy markers, teach-back usage, and interruption rates. Feedback dashboard available by the time the student leaves the room.

Cross-industry precedent (canonical):
- FLAIM Systems: Builds firefighter training rigs where the suit heats up, the breathing apparatus restricts airflow, and a VR headset shows the fire. Trainees were four times more engaged than with screen-only training. One trainee reported forgetting she was in a simulation. The technology did not take over; it made the sensory environment convincing enough that the body responded as if the conditions were real.
- UCSD Medicine: When learners could not always get to the sim center, the team built portable simulation backpacks that brought the training to clinical sites instead.

Architectural specs: Power 10-15 W/sqft. Data: ceiling sensors, ambient intelligence, large monitors. Square footage slightly increased. Acoustics standard. HVAC standard. Structural standard. Cost premium +10-15%.

Degrade: Falls back to Level 1 by powering off sensors and displays.

**Level 3: Tech in-the-Loop, Co-Participant (+30-50%)**

AI-assisted continuous micro-assessment during team scenarios. Real-time performance tracking. Cloud-based distributed simulation. The physical space stays constant while training content evolves via software.

Training examples: AI-tracked surgical performance where the instrument measures tremor, economy of motion, and tissue tension in real time (the learner operates, the AI keeps score, the attending debriefs with evidence). Team crisis scenarios with live assessment, such as a full code-blue simulation with AI tracking closed-loop communication, time-to-intervention, and role overlap, with feedback landing in debrief minutes after the event. Branching clinical decision sims that reshape themselves around the learner's choices (ordering the wrong lab changes what the patient looks like thirty minutes later).

Research voices:
- Korndorffer's vision: AI-assisted continuous micro-assessment during team scenarios. "We can't define competency, articulate it, measure it, that's our problem." AI tracks patterns to help close that gap.
- Coker: Robotic surgical data shows expert users have "much tighter" arm movement patterns than novices. "At least you can track improvement." But nobody has studied whether these metrics correlate to actual surgical competence.
- Gibson on evaluation: AI could help with structured assessment, but "it would take a lot of human work on the rubric."
- Tech Futures Theme, Longitudinal Performance via Game Mechanics and Biometric Feedback: Moving beyond binary pass/fail to "campaign mode" with continuous playback of performance, digital portfolios tracking decision-making over time.
- Secondary research, Digital Learner Twin: Longitudinal, dynamic model of developing competencies. Aggregates data from exam scores, simulation performance, ambient intelligence feedback, and patient outcomes. Predicts struggles before failure occurs.

Cross-industry precedent (canonical):
- Maritime: Cloud-based distributed simulation runs bridge crews across continents on the same scenario.
- FAA: 50+ years of certification standards correlated with fatal-accident reduction in commercial aviation.
- Robotics: Every actuation is logged; longitudinal skill curves are now a standard credentialing artifact.

Architectural specs: Power 20-30 W/sqft (heavy server load). Data: server racks, multi hi-fi screens, sensor arrays. Square footage substantial expansion. Acoustics moderate (sensor accuracy required). HVAC increased (server heat dissipation). Structural standard. Cost premium +30-50%.

Prerequisite (competency definition vacuum): AI assessment at this level depends on being able to define what the AI is measuring. The rubric work has not been completed in medical education. Flag as prerequisite, not blocker.

Degrade: Falls back to Level 2 by disconnecting the cloud / AI layer.

**Level 4: Agent Managed, Directive (+75-100%)**

The room and scenario respond to the learner. Software-defined training environments. Adaptive scenarios that change based on learner actions. AI as scenario director.

Training examples: OSCE 2.0 ambiguity scenarios where the AI adjusts patient affect, symptom evolution, and distractor events to target the individual learner's weak spots (no two runs are the same). VR pre-procedure training where a surgeon rehearses this patient's anatomy from their actual imaging the night before, with the room loading the scenario from the EHR. Adaptive scenario environments where walls, lighting, sounds, and props reconfigure on a schedule (morning ED trauma bay, afternoon ICU bedside, evening community clinic).

Research voices:
- Concept Garden, Ambiguity Scenarios with Embedded Metrics (OSCE 2.0): High-uncertainty scenarios designed with built-in measurement triggers (critical communication acts, escalation timing, values elicitation), with an upgrade path to adaptive scenarios that change in response to learner actions and LLM-in-the-loop conditions.
- Singer: The room and scenario respond to the learner. AI as scenario director, not just observer.
- AMA Panel (SXSW): Agentic AI is the emerging frontier. Every physician is projected to use AI within 5 years. 80%+ already use it for documentation, chart summarization, and diagnosis support.

Cross-industry precedent (canonical):
- Boeing: VR pre-launch training for crewed spaceflight, where scenarios respond to the pilot's choices in real time.
- Oil & Gas: Point-of-need simulation runs on actual rigs, not simulators, moving the training to the hazard.

Architectural specs: Power 40-60 W/sqft (dedicated voltage). Data: edge compute, VR/AR, dynamic wall power. Square footage maximum flexible. Acoustics significant (multi-channel audio). HVAC significant (compute heat load). Structural standard. Cost premium +75-100%.

Prerequisite: All Level 3 prerequisites plus curriculum integration. The AI-directed scenario has to map to what the program actually teaches.

Degrade: Falls back to Level 3 by switching from AI-directed to human-directed scenarios.

**Level 5: Agentic Peer, Embodied (+150%+)**

AI acts as a physical participant: not a tool, not an observer. The "diamond arrangement" places the patient, family, provider, and technology at equal eye level as equal participants in the conversation. Showcase only: one or two rooms maximum.

Training examples: Embodied AI team-member scenarios where the AI is physically present (a screen on a mobile base, a projected presence, or a humanoid) at equal eye level with the team and addressed by name. Trust calibration exercises where the AI is deliberately given intermittent errors, with the training target being the learner's ability to detect, challenge, and override without social cost. Co-creative clinical decision-making where diagnosis is built jointly, the learner articulates reasoning out loud, the AI offers a differential, and together they narrow it (measurement is on the dialogue, not the outcome).

Research voices:
- Singer's embodied AI concept: AI acts as a physical participant in the room.
- Tech Futures Theme, AI as a Collaborative Team Member: Future clinical environments must transition from viewing technology as a tool to viewing it as a peer participant. Strategic planning should adopt a "diamond arrangement" framework.
- Tech Futures Theme: Training must emphasize teaming with AI agents, managing trust calibration, and overseeing autonomous systems rather than just operating equipment.

Cross-industry precedent (canonical):
- Soul Paint, SXSW: VR as co-creative partner. Technology was a participant in the creative process, not a channel for the final product.
- Spurs Athletics: The Human Performance Lab reframed measurement as celebration rather than surveillance.

Architectural specs: Power 80+ W/sqft (industrial / quantum-ready). Data: quantum / HPC clusters, dedicated local compute. Square footage maximum + vertical clearance. Acoustics maximum (embodied-AI fidelity). HVAC maximum (reinforced HPC cooling). Structural reinforced floors (heavy compute). Cost premium +150%+.

Prerequisite: All prior prerequisites plus an institutional culture shift. Leadership publicly models data transparency, performance dashboards are opt-in and learner-owned, and AI agents are introduced as team members in faculty development before they appear in student training.

Degrade: Falls back to Level 4 by switching embodied AI to screen-based AI.

**Graceful degradation ladder.** Every room above Level 1 must be able to step down. Power off the sensor layer, switch to analog controls, disconnect the cloud, swap the agent for a screen, and the room still serves hospital or learner needs. Level 5 falls to 4, 4 to 3, 3 to 2, 2 to 1. Level 1 is the fallback.

### III. ROOM ZERO, THE PIPES NOT FAUCETS PRINCIPLE

Room Zero is the framework's most strategically important output: the over-provisioned baseline infrastructure that goes in EVERY room at construction, regardless of the room's intended day-one level. Specific technology choices made now will be obsolete by opening day, but architectural decisions (power, data conduit, structural reinforcement) are locked in at construction. Over-provisioning the pipes makes future upgrades a software exercise rather than a capital project.

Research voices supporting Room Zero:
- Korndorffer, flexibility is lesson #1: "What we build in three years is not necessarily going to be what needs to be done five years hence." His experience at Tulane and Stanford: "Once you build it, if you're stuck, then what?"
- Murray, "you'll be wrong": "Predicting the future is pretty easy. Predicting it well is really, really hard. One thing I'll guarantee, you'll be wrong." Establish a wide baseline rather than betting on specific technologies.
- Singer, spaces feel dated by opening day: Arizona State's Decision Theater (staff dress as futuristic, tech is five years behind), and a military cybersecurity center modeled on the Star Trek Enterprise with sliding doors that whoosh for no reason. The centers that try to look futuristic are the ones that fail most spectacularly.
- Broderick, facilities-management pain: Power requirements change every time new equipment arrives. This is a recurring operational headache when infrastructure wasn't over-provisioned.
- SOF Trainer: "Incredibly expensive, hard to manage, lots of hardware. It dates quickly." Simplicity and modularity beat complexity.

**Six systems for minimum viable flexibility.** Each system is specified at build time so the room is never trapped at its day-one level.

01. POWER, conduit for L3 demand at minimum. Run enough electrical conduit to carry the room to Level 3 even if it opens at Level 1. In practice: install 40-60 watts per square foot of empty conduit now. Pulling new wire through an occupied building later costs 4-6x the money and takes the room offline.

02. DATA, network wiring that grows without rip-out. Treat the network like plumbing, not a vendor product; it belongs in the walls. In practice: fiber connections at every zone, with backup paths built in. No single vendor's system locking you in.

03. WALLS, attachment points and movable partitions. A room can switch roles over a weekend, not a fiscal quarter. In practice: metal rail (Unistrut or similar) every 16 inches, with partition tracks at the floor slab. Layout changes do not require a contractor.

04. ACOUSTICS, sound isolation rated to STC 50. STC 50 means a normal conversation next door is inaudible. In practice: walls floor to ceiling with sound-absorbing insulation and vibration-dampening channels. Ceiling microphones for ambient intelligence and embodied AI both need a quiet baseline to work.

05. HVAC, capacity for added compute heat. Size the cooling loop now for Level 4 server heat loads, even in Level 1 rooms. In practice: adding cooling capacity after the building opens means roof work, chiller replacements, and shutdown windows.

06. STRUCTURAL, floor loading headroom for future equipment. Cadaver tanks, force-feedback surgical rigs, and server cabinets all assume the floor can take it. In practice: design slabs to hold at least 125 pounds per square foot, exceeding IBC minimums for medical occupancies.

**What Room Zero is NOT.**
- Not the tech itself. Room Zero is the conduit, not the cable; the slab, not the server.
- Not a Level. A Level 1 room with Room Zero is still a Level 1 room until it is populated.
- Not optional. Ambulatory and skills-lab facets need the same backup, or they strand out of the portfolio.

**Cost reference.** Room Zero at build time adds roughly 8-12% to room shell cost. Retrofitting the same capability into an occupied building runs 3-5x for Level 3 capability and 6-9x for Level 4. The cheapest room is the one you never have to rebuild.

**The rule of thumb.** Every room gets Room Zero. The intended level only determines what is populated inside it.

### IV. THE COST STEP

Cost premium relative to Level 1 (directional, validated against AEC benchmarks):
- L1: 1x (baseline)
- L2: ~1.5x
- L3: ~3x
- L4: ~6x
- L5: ~9x

Between Level 3 and Level 4 the premium roughly doubles. Past this line, rooms stop sharing building infrastructure and need their own dedicated power, edge computing, and cooling. The same line is also a curriculum gate: L4 rooms are AI-directed, and if the program has not defined what the AI is measuring, the room sits empty no matter what was spent on it. While the framework maps infrastructure requirements, levels 3-5 depend on curriculum commitments that must develop in parallel.

### V. THE THREE TRAPS

The three failure modes the framework is designed to prevent.

01. OVER-SPEC, the Specification Trap. Over-specified rooms that try to look futuristic feel dated by opening day and end up being remembered as a waste of resources. Singer's Arizona State Decision Theater is the canonical example.

02. STRANDED CAPACITY, the Fantasy Trap. Building capability nobody uses. Team-training rooms that were "never used for that" (Coker's lesson from UCSD).

03. UNDER-SPEC, the Stranded-Asset Trap. Under-specified rooms that become immovable when needs evolve. Retrofit cost eats the original savings three times over (3-5x at Level 3, 6-9x at Level 4).

### VI. THE HUB / SPOKE / ANYWHERE MODEL (COMPANION TO THE MATRIX)

The Tech Readiness Matrix maps WHAT INFRASTRUCTURE each room needs. The Building the System concept maps WHERE training happens.

Hub (the dedicated physical center): reserved over time for experiences that genuinely require physical presence, including team crisis simulation, haptic procedures, embodied AI partners.

Spoke (training near clinical work): communication training, individual assessment, just-in-time skill warm-up. Modeled on Broderick's UCSD simulation backpacks.

Anywhere (remote, virtual, on-demand): cognitive rehearsal, communication practice, individual assessment that can happen on a phone, laptop, home environment, bedside, or in transit.

The principle: the hub holds only what must be physical. Each training type has a different threshold for when it can leave the hub. The matrix tells planners what infrastructure each location needs as that distribution shifts over the building's lifetime.

Supporting evidence:
- Coker (haptic story): First time on the robot, she "annihilated the suture" because of no haptic feedback. "The robot always wins. The weakest thing will break." Real tissue experience is irreplaceable; this is what stays in the hub.
- Murray: Tactile fidelity is non-negotiable. "The thing you reach over for has to feel just like the instrument you would use in real life." This stays in the hub.
- Broderick: "It's hard to beat anything that's hands-on." Individual cognitive and communication skills can migrate outward.
- Singer: Distributed simulation works. Quality can be decoupled from geography for many training types.

### VII. WHAT THE MATRIX MUST ACCOUNT FOR, FRICTION POINTS AND RISKS

The Competency Definition Vacuum (Korndorffer / Coker): "We can't define competency, articulate it, measure it." Every AI-powered assessment level (3+) depends on being able to define what the AI is measuring. The matrix flags this as a prerequisite for higher levels.

The Economic Barrier (Korndorffer): Simulation training "is not a required part of your job. There is no money that's occurring because you're not treating patients." Higher-level rooms cost more to build AND more to staff. The matrix needs cost-of-retrofit vs. cost-of-over-provision analysis to justify the premium.

Curriculum is out of scope but everything depends on it: Insights 1, 3, 7, and 8 from the synthesis all require curriculum commitments. The matrix can recommend infrastructure, but without curriculum backing the spaces sit empty.

AI failures are real (SXSW Nurses panel): AI scheduling assigned unsafe nurse-to-patient ratios. AI recommended dangerous fluid levels. Systems hallucinated IV drug use from "pine needles." Newborn labs showed adult reference ranges. The matrix must ensure every level above Unplugged has a graceful degradation path back to Level 1.

Cognitive deskilling risk (AMA Panel): Risk of physicians becoming overly reliant on AI. GPS dependency analogy. Medical students already uncomfortable with their AI dependency. The matrix must preserve Level 1 capability as a permanent, non-negotiable feature, not a legacy holdover.

### VIII. ARCHITECTURAL SPEC DIMENSIONS THE MATRIX INCLUDES

Each level has specific specifications across these dimensions:

Power: L1 5 W/sqft (standard outlets), L2 10-15 W/sqft, L3 20-30 W/sqft (heavy server load), L4 40-60 W/sqft (dedicated voltage), L5 80+ W/sqft (industrial / quantum-ready).

Data infrastructure: L1 basic networking and wall outlets, L2 ceiling sensors and ambient intelligence with large monitors, L3 server racks and multi hi-fi screens with sensor arrays, L4 edge compute and VR/AR with dynamic wall power, L5 quantum / HPC clusters with dedicated local compute.

Square footage: L1 minimal, L2 slightly increased, L3 substantial expansion, L4 maximum flexible, L5 maximum plus vertical clearance.

Acoustic treatment: L1-2 standard, L3 moderate (sensor-accuracy required), L4 significant (multi-channel audio), L5 maximum (embodied-AI fidelity).

HVAC capacity: L1-2 standard, L3 increased (server heat dissipation), L4 significant (compute heat load), L5 maximum (reinforced HPC cooling).

Structural: L1-4 standard, L5 reinforced floors (heavy compute).

Cost premium over base: L1 0%, L2 +10-15%, L3 +30-50%, L4 +75-100%, L5 +150%+.

### 1. Dr. Kimberly Brown
_Surgical oncologist; Associate Chair of Education, Dell Med_

**Headline takeaway.** Why simulation is no longer optional for a 2026-era medical school, and why funding has to come from clinical revenue.

**Summary.**
Dr. Brown anchored the conversation in the realities of collaborative care for vulnerable Central Texas patients and the gaps that current medical education leaves unaddressed. She described how multidisciplinary care actually works at the Cancer Care Collaborative at Seton, where it works, and where the system breaks down, particularly when students rotate through specialties separately and never see a coordinated team in motion. She made a strong case that simulation is no longer optional for a 2026-era medical school, that Dell Med is behind the standards set by peers like Harvard, UT Southwestern, and UTMB, and that the center’s funding has to be tied to clinical revenue rather than philanthropy if it is going to last. Her perspective on assessment was direct. Confidence and competence are not the same, and a culture focused on validation rather than learning is producing students who fear mistakes more than they pursue mastery.

**Key points:**
- The Cancer Care Collaborative at Seton serves uninsured and underinsured patients with built-in support systems, including Spanish-speaking navigators, Lyft vouchers, HEB gift cards, and hotel vouchers, plus a Social Determinants of Health questionnaire for every patient.
- Almost every patient needs radiation consultation, but radiation services are not in-system. The gap forces external partnerships and creates communication breakdowns across separate locations.
- Students rotate through specialties separately (internal medicine, surgery, ob-gyn, psych) and may never experience collaborative care depending on which assignments they draw.
- Competence has to be observed multiple times against clear, agreed-upon success criteria. Trained evaluators must be able to articulate specific deficiencies down to needle angle, force, and knot-tying technique.
- The current student culture is focused on validation rather than learning. Helicopter parenting has produced a fear of mistakes and a grade-focused mindset that treats every error as an existential threat.
- Every other comparable medical school has a simulation center. Dell Med is so far behind that current passive learning is creating inequality, with strong students getting richer in opportunity and struggling students falling further behind.
- The sustainability model has to tie simulation costs to clinical revenue, making it part of the cost of delivering care rather than a one-time philanthropic line item.
- Medical education should start much earlier. Middle school exposure, then high school and college work-study pathways, with viable career exits at every stage so no one ends up in debt without prospects.

### 2. Dr. James R. Korndorffer Jr.
_Vice Dean of Education, Dell Medical School_

**Headline takeaway.** Build for change, not for ribbon-cutting. The strategic frame for the project, from a builder of two simulation centers.

**Summary.**
Dr. Korndorffer set the strategic frame for the project: build for change, not for what looks impressive at ribbon-cutting. Drawing on his experience founding the Tulane Center for Advanced Medical Simulation and directing Stanford’s Goodman Surgical Simulation Center, he treated flexibility as the non-negotiable design lesson. What gets built in three to five years is not what will be needed five years after that, and the center has to absorb change without capital-intensive rebuilds. He argued for competency-based education over time-based requirements, pushed hard on the distinction between confidence and competence, and pointed to the structural reasons simulation has not scaled in medicine the way it has in aviation. His framing of the project as preparing learners for a ‘hospital of the future’ that may be transformed by AI and quantum computing set the long-horizon brief the team carried forward.

**Key points:**
- Flexibility is the top priority for the new facility. Technology and needs will change dramatically in three to five years, and the center cannot be locked into current design assumptions.
- AI and quantum computing may completely transform training methods. Storage, infrastructure, and ‘hospital of the future’ goals all need to be designed for now.
- Team-based training is currently siloed despite medicine being a team sport. Students train individually but work collaboratively in practice, and the realistic environments needed to bridge that gap do not yet exist.
- The cultural barriers to simulation adoption are structural, not philosophical. Simulation produces no immediate revenue, it is not mandated the way aviation simulator hours are, and staff have to be paid for non-patient-care activities.
- Confidence versus competence is the assessment problem. Self-efficacy is measurable and meaningful in a way that general confidence is not, and expert observation remains the best method despite being extremely time-intensive.
- Micro-assessments (short three-question formats given repeatedly with multiple evaluators) beat single comprehensive twenty-question batteries for both compliance and accuracy.
- Competency-based education should replace time-based requirements. AI is reducing the burden of memorization and freeing curriculum to focus on application, soft skills, communication, clinical reasoning, and teamwork.
- Simulation is essential, not optional, for competency-based education. No one should perform a key procedure on a patient for the first time without first being trained and checked off in a sim center against clear criteria.

### 3. Lucas Artusi
_Systems and service designer, Apple; formerly Design Institute for Health, IDEO_

**Headline takeaway.** Technology in clinical and learning environments has to be treated as another person in the room, not a barrier between provider and patient.

**Summary.**
Lucas brought the design-first lens to the work, the kind of perspective an outsider with deep medical-design experience would notice that insiders would miss. His central argument was that technology in clinical and learning environments has to be treated as ‘another person in the room,’ an equal participant in the patient-provider conversation, arranged at eye level rather than as a barrier. He drew a sharp line between elevated technology (visible to the patient, part of the conversation) and invisible technology (background enablement that does not interrupt the relationship), and warned that current EHRs fail this test badly. On AI in medical education, he was sober. Today’s LLMs are likely close to plateaued, the hallucination problem is not getting solved, and the right move is to focus on what AI can actually do today (translation, sentiment analysis, scaling mock-patient practice) rather than tomorrow’s promises. He offered the team a practical research tool: plot every problem on multiple spectrums (tangible/intangible, training/practice, immersive/invisible, high-touch/low-touch, utilitarian/hedonic) to narrow scope and avoid trying to solve everything at once.

**Key points:**
- Technology should function as ‘another person in the room,’ with patient, family, provider, and technology arranged in a diamond at eye level as equal participants in the conversation.
- There are two distinct categories of healthcare tech: elevated technology (visible to patients, part of the conversation) and invisible technology (background enablement that does not create a provider-patient barrier).
- Current EHRs create a gulf between patients and providers. They are too complex and clinician-facing for shared viewing, and providers themselves resist the transparency that would make them shareable.
- Current AI capabilities have likely plateaued. LLMs will not get significantly smarter, the inherent hallucination problem will not be solved, and the design move is to focus on what AI can do today, not future promises.
- Promising near-term AI applications include medical translation for diverse patient populations, sentiment analysis during patient interactions, real-time feedback on communication tone, and scaling mock-patient conversations to ten times current practice volume.
- Power asymmetry concerns matter. Patients are already disadvantaged in the healthcare system, so any AI tool needs explicit consent, an ‘off the record’ option, and clear benefit to patients themselves rather than just providers.
- A practical research framing tool: plot the problem on multiple spectrums (tangible/intangible, training/practice, immersive/invisible, high-touch/low-touch, utilitarian/hedonic) to narrow the problem space.
- The simulation center’s primary focus should be preserving the patient-provider relationship and addressing the soft-skills gap in medical education, including communication across health-literacy levels and cultural competency.

### 4. Pilar Gormley
_Nurse educator, Georgetown / MedStar system_

**Headline takeaway.** The operational ground truth of what makes a sim center actually get used, from inside a working academic medical center.

**Summary.**
Pilar gave the team an operational ground-truth view from inside a working academic medical center’s simulation program. Her lens was nurse education and continuing competency, and her concerns were the ones that determine whether a sim center actually gets used: space, scheduling, staffing, maintenance, and clinician involvement in design. She described what works at Georgetown, including the ‘hot topics’ annual competency validation in three-to-four-hour hands-on sessions, multi-disciplinary participation that exposes different specialty approaches, and spacing training across the year rather than front-loading. She also described what fails: rooms too small for the staff volumes that need them, equipment that breaks faster than it gets fixed, only four to six hours of availability some days because of scheduling conflicts. Residents are already scoring around forty percent on specialty exams while ChatGPT scores higher, and the risk is not that AI replaces clinicians but that newer graduates lose the clinical judgment to catch when the algorithm is wrong on an outlier case.

**Key points:**
- Working in nurse education at the Georgetown / MedStar system, the recurring constraints on simulation are size, technology limits, competing priorities for space, four-to-six-hour availability windows due to scheduling conflicts, and equipment maintenance issues.
- The ‘hot topics’ annual competency validation, with three-to-four-hour hands-on sessions spaced across the year, works far better than overwhelming front-loaded training.
- Multi-disciplinary participation in simulation matters because it surfaces how different specialties approach the same scenario.
- Critical success factors for a new center: adequate space, sufficient staffing, scenarios realistic to the actual facility’s capabilities, and strong clinician involvement in the design process from the start.
- Healthcare systems are slow to adapt new technology and need interoperability between systems.
- Physicians are already using AI for dictation and billing. Medical residents have scored around forty percent on specialty exams compared to ChatGPT.
- The risk is not replacement but erosion of clinical judgment. Newer graduates may lack the hands-on patient interaction needed to catch the outlier case where the algorithm does not fit.
- Current medical education front-loads classroom time before patient exposure, the helicopter-parent generation struggles with failure, and discipline silos persist. Simulation has to address all three.

### 5. Dr. Alisa Coker
_Director of Robotic Surgery and Education, Dell Medical School_

**Headline takeaway.** The operational ground truth of training residents on borrowed equipment. Identity formation, not just skill acquisition.

**Summary.**
Dr. Coker is the operational ground truth of the project. **Key points:**

- Real tissue experience is critical. Pig models are expensive but necessary; virtual simulators help muscle memory but are insufficient alone.

Isolated task practice is too boring to sustain repetition unless the experience is wrapped in a meaningful outcome. Training should be designed around outcomes like ‘save the patient,’ not decontextualized procedural checklists, with leaderboards, scoring, and goals that drive practice when no instructor is watching. He pushed the team toward team-based simulation. Code situations are a team sport, and team training does not require maximal technical complexity; low-tech mannequins and simpler setups are often sufficient for orchestration practice. His most pointed contribution was on data: if you do not capture the learner’s profile longitudinally, why bother? Individual performance tracking over time should be core to the design, not an afterthought.

**Key points:**
- The widget is not the point. The simulation center should be designed as an end-to-end ecosystem that helps a human learn, not a collection of flashy devices.
- Repetitions matter. The lab should prioritize volume of practice over realism or novelty.
- Build a continuum of access. Low-fidelity practice via phone, laptop, or game console at home; facility-based simulation in the middle; cadaveric or real-world practice at the top.
- A lab-only strategy limits scale. Digital access expands training throughput beyond what physical hardware allows.
- Design training around outcomes, not procedural checklists. The scenario is ‘save the patient,’ and the technical reps live inside a meaningful clinical mission.
- Medical emergencies are a team sport. The lab should train coordination, communication, and team execution alongside individual technical skill.
- Humans only learn when they are motivated to do it internally. Gamification (leaderboards, scoring, visible feedback) drives self-directed practice when faculty are not watching.
- Capturing a learner’s longitudinal performance profile should be core to the lab’s design, not an afterthought. Without persistent attribution and honest feedback, the data has no value.

### 21. Future Learners
_Premed undergraduates; composite voice from 10+ UT Austin interviews_

**Headline takeaway.** A composite of 10+ premed undergraduates: futuristic sim centers read as a red flag. Messy and in use is the credibility signal.

**Summary.**
This entry synthesizes themes from interviews with ten or more UT Austin premed undergraduates. Names and identifying details have been anonymized for privacy. The students described their existing mental model of a simulation center as sterile and museum-like, places they would be terrified of breaking something rather than environments where they would actually train. Their strongest signal was that a futuristic-looking sim center reads as a red flag: it suggests the school cares more about donor photoshoots than functional education. The credibility signal they look for instead is messy and in use, a resident sweating through a scenario or students running reps on their own time. They asked for a formalized system for failure, separated from grading, with immediate empathetic debriefing. They want handling uncertainty taught as a skill in itself, ahead of procedures or pharmacology, framed as inoculation against panic. They want emotional decompression space, the kind of dedicated room described in the report as the Porch, instead of being left to cry in stairwells. On AI, they were measured: it should function as a super-scribe and invisible assistant, not a decision-maker, and AI patients are only useful if their narrative fidelity reaches the point of getting defensive, lying about history out of fear, or crying. Their biggest stated fear about training is the loss of empathy. The second is the Level 1 failure mode, becoming dependent on AI tools and losing the baseline clinical intuition that has to be there when systems go down. They also flagged that undergrad creates strict silos between premeds, nursing students, and other future health professionals, and that interprofessional training has to start before clerkships if it is going to feel real.

**Key points:**
- The current mental model of a sim center is sterile, museum-like, white walls and pristine mannequins, a place to be afraid of breaking something. The new center has to break this perception or it will not get used.
- A pristine, futuristic-looking sim center reads as a red flag, a donor-photoshoot building rather than functional education. The credibility signal they look for is messy and in use.
- The Sim Gym concept resonates. Students running reps on their own time, because they actually want to be there, is the cultural signal that distinguishes a working center from a showpiece.
- Handling uncertainty is the single most valuable skill they want a sim center to teach, ahead of procedures, communication, or teamwork. Inoculation against panic is the explicit ask.
- Failure has to be formalized, separated from grading, and met with immediate empathetic debriefing. If the lab evaluates them on mistakes, they will play it safe and never take risks.
- Team training has to be in person. Non-verbal cues, sensing stress, eye contact, knowing when to speak up versus stay out of the way, none of that simulates over video.
- AI is welcome as a super-scribe and invisible assistant. It is not welcome as a decision-maker. The Level 1 failure fear: becoming dependent on AI tools and losing the baseline clinical intuition that is needed when systems go down.
- Emotional decompression space (the Porch concept) is named as a game-changer. The current alternative is crying in stairwells. Loss of empathy is the single biggest stated fear about training.

### 2. SXSW Innovation

**Summary.** Three panels at SXSW gave the team a cross-industry read on where healthcare technology is being negotiated in public. The conversations cut across agentic AI in medical decision-making, the role of nurses as the human firewall against automation, and the use of immersive play and ritual as therapeutic interventions. Together they framed the questions the simulation center has to answer about AI integration, identity preservation, and humanization of technology.

**Observations:**
- Panel one, "The future of healthcare is at home" with Ricky Choi (Samsung), Dhruv Kumar, John Whyte (AMA), and Arundhati Parmar. Physician AI usage doubled since 2023, with over 80 percent now using AI for documentation, chart summarization, and diagnostic support. Within five years every physician will use AI in some capacity.
- Cognitive deskilling is the central concern. The current generation of medical students will train fundamentally differently, with comparison drawn to GPS dependency. One student described forcing himself to complete a differential diagnosis independently before consulting AI.
- AI prescribing controversy: Utah’s Doctronic pilot allows AI prescribing for renewals. The AMA opposes prescribing without human oversight, citing slippery-slope concerns and a documented case where researchers tricked the bot into prescribing three times the proper dosage.
- Wearables are moving from fitness tracking to medical detection. Atrial fibrillation, sleep apnea, ADHD management through haptic reminders. The institutional motivation is to remove friction between detection and treatment, not just identify problems.
- Hospital-at-home programs are limited geographically but expanding. Patients prefer their own beds, family proximity, and continuous data generation over hospital admission for stable conditions requiring IV antibiotics or similar.
- Panel two, "Nurses as firewall between AI and patients." Union-organizing context at Mission Regional Medical Center after acquisition by HCA. Cost-cutting, staffing reductions, and shareholder-first culture have made nurses the last human checkpoint between hospital systems and patient harm.
- Panel three, "Technologies of care: ritual play and the future of wellness" with Sarin (Hatsumi) and Nikki (Soul Paint). Demonstration of Soul Paint, a VR body-mapping experience, and Deep, a meditative breathing-controlled game. Both are deployed clinically with published research showing improved emotional regulation and reduced PTSD symptoms in youth detention populations.
- Core argument from the wellness panel: the healthcare system is fragmented along the human experience itself, mental health separated from physical, expression separated from work. Embodied play and ritual are the actionable mechanisms (not the spiritual framing) that historically reconnect these. The center should design for reconnection, not just skill acquisition.
- The XR Health Alliance reports 20 million GBP UK government investment in immersive health products since 2021. The arts-and-health field is finally generating clinical evidence at scale, with WHO, UNESCO, and the NHS all formally engaged.
